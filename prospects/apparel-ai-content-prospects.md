# Apparel Prospect List — AI Social Content System

**Offer:** a monthly social media content system for apparel brands, led with AI-generated
images of models wearing their actual products (plus captions, posting calendar, and
short-form video cuts).

**Why this list:** every brand here sells direct-to-consumer online, drops new SKUs faster
than they can afford to shoot them, and is small enough that the founder is reachable
without going through a procurement process. That combination is the whole qualification
test — big retail groups have in-house studios, and heritage/craft labels sell the
photograph itself, so neither belongs on a first list.

**How to work it:** do not cold-pitch a deck. Pull 2–3 of their existing flat-lay or
on-body product shots, generate a set of model images from them, and send those. The
image *is* the pitch. Everything below is written assuming that.

---

## Tier A — Fastest yes: high SKU turnover, model shots are non-negotiable for sales

Activewear and swimwear cannot sell from a flat-lay. The customer is buying fit. These
brands already know they need on-body imagery for every colourway, and the cost of that
scales linearly with their range — which is exactly the pain the system removes.

### 1. Kheper Athleisure — Cape Town
- Women's activewear, founded 2017 by Mena and Martin; manufactures ~99% in its own
  Cape Town factory. Built explicitly around size inclusivity, "women of all shapes and sizes."
- **Why they fit:** inclusivity is their stated positioning, which means every style
  ideally needs to be shown on multiple body types — the single most expensive thing to
  shoot conventionally and the single cheapest thing to generate. Own factory = constant
  new colourways.
- **Hook:** "You say activewear for every woman. Here's your current bestseller on four
  different bodies, shot in one afternoon of compute."
- **Route:** site contact form / IG DM (@kheper_sa on X, same handle pattern on IG).

### 2. KOKO ACTIVE — South Africa
- "Luxurious activewear, locally made, ethically designed and made by women, for women."
- **Why they fit:** premium price point but small-brand budget — the gap between how they
  want to look and what a shoot costs is the widest here.
- **Hook:** editorial-grade campaign imagery at the frequency their drop schedule needs.
- **Route:** kokoactive.co.za contact page.

### 3. Livv Activewear — South Africa
- Founded 2021. Sports bras, leggings, jumpsuits, sets — sold online only.
- **Why they fit:** young brand, wide SKU/colour range, no legacy studio relationship to
  displace. Sets and jumpsuits especially need on-body context.
- **Route:** livvactivewear.co.za.

### 4. Boost Gymwear — South Africa
- Ladies' gymwear custom-made locally, with fit, colour **and length** options per garment.
- **Why they fit:** this is the strongest technical fit on the list. Made-to-order variants
  multiply the image count beyond what any shoot budget can cover — they physically cannot
  photograph every combination they sell. Generation is the only way that catalogue gets
  covered.
- **Hook:** "Every length and colour combination you offer, shown on a model. All of them."
- **Route:** boostgymwear.co.za.

### 5. SÁLTI Swimwear — Cape Town
- Handmade in South Africa, fabrics sourced locally in Cape Town.
- **Why they fit:** swimwear is seasonal and shoot-dependent. Small handmade operation =
  no chance of funding a beach shoot per drop, but the category demands lifestyle imagery
  more than any other.
- **Caution:** "handmade" brands care about authenticity — lead with the hybrid workflow
  (see Positioning below), not with "AI models."
- **Route:** saltiswimwear.co.za.

### 6. Leah Marie Swim — Cape Town
- Handmade bikini tops, bottoms and sets, sold online.
- **Why they fit:** mix-and-match separates means the number of sellable combinations far
  exceeds anything they can shoot. Same argument as Boost, different category.
- **Route:** leahmarie.co.za.

### 7. Granadilla Swim — Cape Town
- Founded 2014 on Clifton 4th by Adam Duxbury and Joshua Meltz. Swim shorts, bikinis,
  kiddies, accessories; bold prints; recycled polyester and organic cotton. Has a US-facing
  arm (@granadilla.usa) alongside the SA account.
- **Why they fit:** the biggest and best-resourced target here, and the one with the
  clearest expansion problem — running SA and US accounts means needing two distinct
  content streams (different seasons, different casting, different locations) off one
  product range. That is the strongest commercial case on this list.
- **Hook:** "Northern-hemisphere summer content for the US account, in December, without
  flying anyone anywhere."
- **Caution:** they are sustainability-forward and already have real brand photography.
  Sell this as extending their existing look into more placements, not replacing it.
- **Route:** granadillaswim.com contact page; LinkedIn company page is active.

## Tier B — Streetwear: sell the campaign, not the catalogue

These brands don't need catalogue coverage, they need a *look*. The pitch shifts from
"cheaper than a shoot" to "campaign concepts you couldn't afford to stage" — a model in
a location, a lighting setup, a scenario. Higher creative bar, better margin if it lands.

### 8. We Are Gods — South Africa
- Premium menswear/streetwear, oversized fits, collections with names like *22* and
  *Humanity To Others*. Publishes its own trend content (streetwear guides, blog).
- **Why they fit:** already investing in content marketing, which means they believe in
  content spend and have someone responsible for it. Collection-led storytelling is
  exactly what generated campaign imagery does well.
- **Route:** wearegodsonline.com.

### 9. Earsplitting FC — Cape Town
- Fan-made football streetwear; made in Cape Town with a 28-year-old local factory.
  Small range (box tees, 5-panel caps), heavy on blog/editorial output.
- **Why they fit:** football culture imagery — terraces, floodlights, tunnel walks — is
  wildly expensive to stage and trivially generated. Their whole identity is a scene they
  can't afford to photograph. Small SKU count also makes this a cheap, fast pilot.
- **Hook:** send them their Saka box tee on a model in a stadium concourse. That single
  image closes it.
- **Route:** earsplitting.co.za.

### 10. BROKE — Cape Town
- Started 2016 by six friends from a Cape Town township; first collection 2020, creative
  director Andile Dlamini. Graphic tracksuits, utility vests, caps — drops reportedly sell
  out fast. Sells at sobroke.online.
- **Why they fit:** drop-model brands need hype imagery *before* stock exists, which is
  the one thing a camera cannot do. Pre-drop teaser content generated off tech packs is a
  genuinely unique offer.
- **Caution:** strong founder-led cultural identity — they will be the most sceptical of AI
  and the most damaged by a leak that it's synthetic. Pitch pre-drop teasers only, keep
  real people in the post-drop content.
- **Route:** sobroke.online; IG DM.

## Positioning — how to sell it without triggering the AI objection

Lead with the hybrid workflow, not the technology:

> One real shoot with one real model. We build a consistent brand model from it, then
> generate every colourway, size variant, seasonal setting and campaign concept from that
> base for the next twelve months. Your model stays your model.

That framing answers the three objections you will hear, in order:

1. **"It won't look like my product."** — Correct, if done from a text prompt. It is done
   from *their* garment photos, and the fit and drape must be checked per image. Say this
   before they do.
2. **"Our customers will hate it."** — Real risk for handmade and founder-led brands
   (SÁLTI, BROKE). The hybrid workflow means the hero imagery is still real; generation
   fills the long tail.
3. **"Is this even allowed?"** — Advertising standards in most markets require that
   imagery not materially mislead about the product. Fit, colour and fabric behaviour must
   be accurate, and disclosure practice is tightening. Build a disclosure line into the
   deliverable and make it a selling point rather than waiting to be asked.

Be straight about the limits in the first meeting. Hands, garment seams, print alignment
across curves and anything worn against skin at close crop are where these images fail.
Every generated set needs a human passing over it before it ships. Sell the review step as
part of the service — it is what separates this from the brand doing it themselves in an
afternoon.

## Suggested outreach sequence

1. **Pick three** — Boost Gymwear, Earsplitting FC, Kheper. Smallest ranges, clearest pain,
   fastest to demo.
2. **Build spec work** — 3 images each, generated from product shots already on their site.
   No deck, no pricing.
3. **Send it cold** — IG DM to the founder, subject line to the effect of *"made these with
   your [product] this morning — free to use, no strings."* Giving the images away is the
   entire conversion mechanism.
4. **Only then** propose the retainer: a monthly volume of images plus captions and a
   calendar, with a paid pilot month before any longer commitment.

Price the pilot against what one shoot costs them (photographer, model, studio, editing),
not against a software subscription — the comparison you invite is the comparison you get
judged on.
